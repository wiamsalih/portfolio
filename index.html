04 / Research

Ongoing inquiry.

Bridging Expertise Gaps: A Comparative Study of AI Accessibility Across User Populations

Author  Wiam Salih Institution  George Mason University Department  Information Sciences & Technology Draft  January 2026
Data collection in progress
Abstract

Conversational AI systems promise to democratize access to complex technical domains, yet designing for accessibility remains challenging when user populations span from complete novices to domain experts. This paper examines AI accessibility through two contrasting case studies — Skylar, an AI agent for cloud infrastructure targeting experienced engineers, and FinBot, a financial literacy chatbot for general audiences — investigating how user expertise shapes interaction patterns, trust formation, and comprehension.

Research question 01
How do design requirements differ across expertise levels?
Comparing what expert cloud engineers need from AI versus what novice middle school students need and why a single system optimized for one fails the other.
Research question 02
How do novices form mental models of AI?
Investigating the questions, misconceptions, and trust patterns that emerge when students ages 11–13 interact with domain-specific conversational AI for the first time.
Research question 03
What principles bridge the expertise spectrum?
Deriving evidence-based design guidelines for building AI systems that serve diverse user populations without sacrificing usability for either end of the spectrum.
Preliminary findings
01
Trust is about agency, not accuracy. Students' most frequent questions were not about whether the AI was correct but about whether they were allowed to disagree with it. This was not anticipated and is reshaping the paper's theoretical framework.
02
Accessibility is not one thing. For experts, it means efficient access to detailed diagnostic data. For novices, it means comprehensible explanations that build foundational knowledge. The same backend succeeds or fails entirely based on how it presents itself.
03
Expertise shapes every design decision. Vocabulary, information density, transparency, and follow-up behavior all require calibration to user background not to a generic "user." This has direct implications for general-purpose AI design.
